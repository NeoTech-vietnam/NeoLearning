## Notes Section: core relationships

A signal carries information about a changing quantity: for example, motor current, shaft displacement or microphone pressure. A sensor/transducer converts a physical quantity into a usable electrical signal. A three-axis accelerometer gives a vector sequence, not just one scalar sequence.

| Representation | Time coordinate | Amplitude |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Analog $x_c(t)$ | Continuous | Continuous |
| Ideal sampled sequence $x[n]$ | Integer index | Can remain continuous-valued |
| Digital sequence $x_q[n]$ | Integer index | Quantized, encoded with finite precision |

$$
x[n]=x_c(nT),\qquad f_s=\frac1T,\qquad \Omega_s=2\pi f_s,\qquad \omega=\Omega T.
$$

$f_s$ is in samples/s (Hz for the sampling clock), $\Omega$ in rad/s, and $\omega$ in rad/sample. Sampling and quantization are distinct operations even when one ADC performs both.

For a uniform $B$-bit converter with nominal full-scale span $V_{\mathrm{span}}$:

$$
K=2^B,\qquad \Delta=\frac{V_{\mathrm{span}}}{2^B},\qquad e[n]=x_q[n]-x[n].
$$

With rounding, no overload and the usual uniform-error approximation:

$$
-\Delta/2\le e[n]\le\Delta/2,\qquad E\{e^2\}\approx\frac{\Delta^2}{12}.
$$

Endpoint conventions vary between converter code maps; the nominal step above divides the full-scale span into $2^B$ intervals. Quantization error is deterministic for a given input; treating it as independent noise is an approximation.

Continuous-time signals are defined for a continuum of times; discrete-time signals are defined at separated instants. An ideal discrete-time sequence does not necessarily have quantized amplitudes.

Use $x(t)$ with real-valued time and $x[n]$ with $n\in\mathbb Z$. A continuous-time signal may be zero over an interval without ceasing to be continuous-time. The integer $n$ is an index; recover physical time using $t=nT$. The sample value may be real, complex or a vector/array of channels.

@@ 7|Analog limitations
Accuracy is limited by component tolerances and undesired nonlinearities. Repeatability suffers from tolerances and environmental changes, including temperature and vibration. Other limitations are electrical noise, bounded voltage/current dynamic range, inflexible changes, difficult nonlinear or time-varying operations, and difficult information storage. These are design tradeoffs rather than a claim that every analog implementation performs poorly.

@@ 10|DSP benefits and costs
Benefits listed are controllable word length/accuracy; repeatability; low sensitivity of digital representations to electrical noise; floating-point dynamic range; software flexibility; easier nonlinear and time-varying operations; cheap storage; encryption; and price/performance and development-time advantages.

Costs are possible sampling information loss, mixed-signal A/D and D/A hardware, finite processor speed, and quantization/roundoff errors. **Clarification:** ideal sampling of a suitably bandlimited signal can preserve information. Digital processing also preserves noise already captured by the ADC; it is not automatically noise-free or BIBO stable.
@@ 11|Analog, interface and digital domains
The diagram separates continuous-time signals/systems and analog electronics from discrete-time numerical processing/digital electronics. ADC and DAC blocks form the interface in opposite directions. Integrals commonly describe continuous-time processing; sums commonly describe discrete-time processing. A complete mechatronic measurement/control chain typically crosses these domains.
@@ 12|Practical conversion chain
Follow analog anti-alias filter → sample-and-hold → ADC → discrete-time system → DAC/hold → reconstruction filter. The first filter limits out-of-band content before sampling; the last suppresses DAC spectral images and may compensate hold distortion. **Source correction:** the upper diagram labels both end filters as avoiding aliasing. At the output, “anti-imaging/reconstruction” is the appropriate purpose.
@@ 13|Anti-aliasing and holding the input
The low-pass anti-alias filter restricts the signal near the folding frequency $f_s/2$. Even a bandlimited useful signal can be accompanied by wideband noise, which would otherwise fold into the measured band. An input that changes during conversion can corrupt a practical ADC result. The switch/capacitor S/H sketch shows acquisition while the switch tracks and charge retention during hold. A practical filter needs transition-band margin below Nyquist.
@@ 14|Sample-and-hold waveform
The S/H circuit samples as nearly instantaneously as practical and holds the sampled voltage nearly constant until the next acquisition. Its idealized output is a staircase. With a fixed sampling clock, the overall continuous-time sampling/holding operation is linear but time-varying: an arbitrary time shift changes its alignment with the clock. A shift by an integer number of clock periods is a special case.
@@ 15|Uniform sampling
Uniform sampling gives $x[n]=x_c(nT)$ for every integer $n$, with period $T$ seconds, frequency $f_s=1/T$, and angular clock frequency $\Omega_s=2\pi f_s$. The stem plot represents values only at the sampling instants. An ideal model has exact times and values; actual ADCs add finite amplitude resolution and practical timing effects.
@@ 16|Sampling is not generally reversible
The same set of samples can lie on several continuous curves, illustrated by different waveforms through the same points. Without constraints the original waveform is ambiguous. Perfect reconstruction requires a suitable signal class, such as a bandlimited signal sampled sufficiently fast. For a low-pass signal with highest frequency $B$, use $f_s>2B$ as the practical strict inequality, with additional filter margin.
@@ 17|ADC stages and quantization
The ADC model separates sampler, $B$-bit quantizer and binary coder. Sampling restricts the time instants; quantization maps continuous amplitudes onto permitted levels; coding represents those levels with bits. The lower graph compares the original curve, ideal samples and quantized samples. Discrete-time theory often ignores finite amplitudes initially; hardware cannot.
@@ 18|Uniform converter codes
A $B$-bit quantizer provides $2^B$ possible codes. Uniform quantization uses equal interval widths $\Delta$ and uniformly spaced reconstruction levels. Distinguish a decision threshold, where a code changes, from the reconstruction level assigned to that code. The two diagrams show a quantizer transfer characteristic, binary labels and the decision/reconstruction levels. Finite precision is the central difference from an ideal converter.
@@ 19|Quantization error and noise power
ADC errors include aliasing and quantization; they arise from different mechanisms. Quantization is nonlinear. With many small quantization intervals, the lecture approximates the signal locally as a straight line, giving a ramp-like error over $-\tau\le t\le\tau$:

$$
e(t)=\frac{\Delta}{2\tau}t,\qquad P_Q=\frac1{2\tau}\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}e^2(t)\,dt=\frac{\Delta^2}{12}.
$$

This statistical/local-linear approximation does not cover clipping, strongly correlated errors, or all very small periodic inputs. The figure shows quantized waveform and error separately.
@@ 20|Ideal and practical DAC pulses
Reconstruction forms a sum of shifted pulses: $x_r(t)=\sum_n x[n]g_r(t-nT)$. The ideal bandlimited pulse is $g_{BL}(t)=\operatorname{sinc}(t/T)$, where $\operatorname{sinc}(v)=\sin(\pi v)/(\pi v)$. A practical switch-and-hold DAC uses $g_{SH}(t)=1$ for $0\le t<T$ and zero otherwise, yielding a staircase from $x_q[n]$.

$$
G_{SH}(j\Omega)=\frac{2\sin(\Omega T/2)}{\Omega}e^{-j\Omega T/2}
=T\operatorname{sinc}\!\left(\frac{\Omega T}{2\pi}\right)e^{-j\Omega T/2}.
$$

The limiting value at $\Omega=0$ is $T$. Its non-flat amplitude response leads into topic 02.

## Lyons supplement: why a sample plot must remain discrete

Lyons emphasizes that the values between sample instants are unspecified by a sequence. Connecting stems with a staircase imposes a hold operation that is not inherent in the sequence itself. ## Worked example: a vibration acquisition channel

**Author-created application of the lecture equations.** Let a nominal 12-bit ADC cover 0-3.3 V and sample at 8 ksample/s.

1. $T=1/8000=125\ \mu\mathrm{s}$.
2. $K=4096$ codes and $\Delta=3.3/4096\approx0.806\ \mathrm{mV}$.
3. Ideal rounding error magnitude is at most about $0.403\ \mathrm{mV}$ without overload; RMS quantization error is $\Delta/\sqrt{12}\approx0.233\ \mathrm{mV}$ under the stated model.
4. A 1 kHz sinusoid has eight samples/cycle and $\omega=\pi/4$ rad/sample.
5. A 7 kHz sinusoid can alias into 1 kHz. More ADC bits will not remove that ambiguity; the analog bandwidth and sampling rate must address it.

## Retrieval practice and answer key

1. Is an ideal sampled signal necessarily digital? **No.** Its amplitudes can remain continuous-valued.
2. What happens to ideal quantization power if one bit is added at fixed span? **It becomes one quarter**, because $\Delta$ halves.
3. Does a staircase prove quantization? **No.** A hold can make a staircase with unquantized heights.
4. Which block must remove a strong out-of-band interferer before it aliases? **The analog anti-alias filter**, with suitable sampling-rate design.

## Summary Section

Sampling chooses times; quantization chooses amplitude levels; coding stores those levels. DSP offers repeatable, programmable processing, but a physical system still needs analog interfaces and must manage bandwidth, finite precision and computation time. A DAC generates pulses whose sum requires reconstruction filtering to approximate the desired analog waveform.
